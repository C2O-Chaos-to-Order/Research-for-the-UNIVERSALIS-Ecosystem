# Golden Compass Research Methods

**Programme:** GC-R02 — Historical Decision-Making and Human Agency  
**Method version:** 0.1.0  
**Status:** Proposed research method for use; not independently validated  
**Document date:** 2026-11-02

## 1. Research purpose

Reconstruct consequential human decisions in their historical and global settings; identify the determinants, institutional conditions, relationships, and information that shaped them; document consequences for all affected parties; and derive carefully bounded prevention and correction requirements.

The research is descriptive and analytical before it is prescriptive. It must not begin with a desired political verdict and select evidence to support it.

## 2. Scope of human agency

Research subjects include, without limitation:

- People exercising political, military, economic, judicial, religious, or institutional authority.
- Advisers, civil servants, researchers, professionals, and intermediaries who shaped decisions.
- Reformers, abolitionists, organizers, dissidents, whistleblowers, and resistance movements.
- Scientists, philosophers, educators, inventors, artists, and knowledge keepers.
- Indigenous nations and other political communities with their own decision processes.
- Workers, families, civilians, displaced people, enslaved people, survivors, witnesses, and other affected populations.
- Collective actors, institutions, and social movements, where the evidence supports treating them as decision-making entities.

Do not equate fame with historical significance, formal office with agency, or a community with a single unanimous viewpoint.

## 3. Mandatory context analysis — GC-R02-GHC

Every case must reconstruct the relevant historical environment across:

1. Domestic political institutions, laws, parties, and constitutional arrangements.
2. Civil society, social movements, inequalities, public opinion, and cultural norms.
3. Economic conditions, trade, labor, resources, fiscal constraints, and distributional effects.
4. Wars, military capacity, security threats, alliances, and strategic geography.
5. Science, technology, communications, transport, and available infrastructure.
6. Religious, intellectual, artistic, and cultural developments.
7. Environmental conditions, epidemics, disasters, agriculture, and material constraints.
8. International relations, imperial systems, colonialism, diplomacy, and independence movements.

Build a contemporaneous timeline for each case. Distinguish events that merely occurred at the same time from events shown by evidence to have influenced the decision. Explain the transmission mechanism before claiming causation. Where evidence is absent, label a global event as contextual rather than causal.

## 4. Mandatory decision determinants — GC-R02-DDA

For each consequential decision, examine:

1. Historical circumstances and the sequence of precipitating events.
2. Information actually available to the decision-maker at the time.
3. Source reliability, intelligence quality, gaps, uncertainty, and misinformation.
4. Personal beliefs, stated values, prior commitments, experiences, and documented biases.
5. Political incentives, electoral pressures, reputation, party competition, and career interests.
6. Advisers, relationships, institutional dependencies, dissent, and whose advice was excluded.
7. Legal authority, institutional constraints, jurisdiction, and procedural requirements.
8. Economic, technological, military, geographic, and logistical constraints.
9. Alternatives considered, alternatives available, and reasons for rejection.
10. Effects on individuals, communities, rights-holders, nonparticipants, and people unable to represent themselves.
11. Warnings, feedback, accountability, correction, reversal, and remedy.

Claims about private motives or psychological states require direct, credible evidence. Do not diagnose historical figures. When evidence supports more than one explanation, preserve the competing explanations.

## 5. Decision reconstruction sequence

Each case study should follow this sequence:

1. Define the decision precisely, including the actor, date range, authority, and action.
2. Establish the contemporaneous global and domestic context.
3. Map the relevant actors, institutions, affected populations, and relationships.
4. Reconstruct what each decision-maker knew, believed, and could reasonably have known at the time.
5. Identify incentives, constraints, dissent, and competing interpretations.
6. Record the options presented, options realistically available, and decision rationale.
7. Trace authorization, implementation, and the roles of other institutions.
8. Document immediate, long-term, intended, unintended, domestic, and international consequences.
9. Examine oversight, legal challenges, accountability, correction, and remedy.
10. Identify plausible preventive safeguards and their limitations.
11. Record unresolved questions and evidence that could change the analysis.

## 6. Three knowledge perspectives

Keep three perspectives separate:

- **Contemporaneous perspective:** Information and constraints available before the decision.
- **Retrospective perspective:** What later documents, investigations, testimony, scholarship, and outcomes established.
- **Alternative-decision perspective:** What other options were realistically available using information accessible at the time.

Do not use later discoveries to imply that earlier actors necessarily knew them. Do not excuse foreseeable harm merely because a practice was common, lawful, or politically accepted in its period. Identify contemporary dissent and alternatives where they existed.

## 7. Affected-party and agency mapping

For each case, identify:

- Who made the decision.
- Who advised, authorized, implemented, resisted, or reviewed it.
- Who benefited and who bore costs or risks.
- Who was excluded from deliberation or denied meaningful consent.
- Who had practical power to contest or correct the decision.
- Which communities had their own institutions, legal traditions, political objectives, and internal disagreements.

Represent Indigenous nations and other communities as political actors, not merely as populations acted upon. Do not infer collective consent from a treaty signature without examining representation, negotiation circumstances, translation, authority, and context.

## 8. Source and claim standards

Use the following source hierarchy as a guide, not as an automatic ranking of truth:

- **Primary records:** Laws, treaties, court records, correspondence, diaries, minutes, orders, census schedules, maps, speeches, contemporary publications, and physical records.
- **Participant and witness testimony:** Firsthand accounts, including later interviews. Assess memory, transcription, translation, editorial mediation, and elapsed time.
- **Institutional and archival collections:** Catalogues, finding aids, scholarly annotations, and curated documentary collections.
- **Secondary scholarship:** Peer-reviewed books and articles, specialist historical studies, and work by scholars connected to the communities under study.
- **Tertiary summaries:** Encyclopedias and general reference accounts, used mainly for orientation and cross-checking.

Primary sources are not inherently neutral. Every source must be evaluated for authorship, date, audience, purpose, provenance, completeness, translation, bias, and relationship to the event.

Classify material as one or more of:
- Documented event or text.
- Contemporary claim or allegation.
- Participant testimony.
- Later recollection.
- Legal holding or official finding.
- Scholarly interpretation.
- Contested interpretation.
- Research hypothesis.
- Unresolved question.

Distinguish a signed document from the claims it contains. A proclamation is evidence of what an administration publicly asserted, not proof that every assertion was accurate.

## 9. Global and comparative standards

Use a worldwide, multi-perspective scope. Avoid treating American or European institutional history as the default measure of all societies. Identify the relevant legal and cultural context without using cultural difference to excuse coercion or erase individual rights.

Compare cases by mechanism and context rather than superficial resemblance. Similar outcomes do not establish identical causes. Avoid partisan rankings and overall scores of political figures. Evaluate specific decisions and processes against transparent criteria.

## 10. Prevention analysis

Translate supported findings into candidate safeguards addressing:

- Legitimate and bounded authority.
- Evidence quality and explicit uncertainty.
- Independent advice, dissent, and review.
- Rights and affected-party impact.
- Proportionality and less harmful alternatives.
- Conflict-of-interest management.
- Transparency appropriate to legitimate confidentiality.
- Reversibility, expiration, and correction.
- Access to appeal, remedy, and independent accountability.
- Equal application to creators, operators, leaders, and systems.

A recommendation must identify the causal mechanism it is intended to interrupt. Do not claim that a safeguard would certainly have prevented a historical outcome unless the evidence supports that counterfactual claim.

## 11. Execution and status control

Maintain separate records for:

- **Discussed:** Appeared in conversation.
- **Proposed:** Recommended for consideration.
- **Accepted:** Explicitly approved by the project owner.
- **Researched:** Sources and analysis were actually examined.
- **Simulated:** A defined simulation was executed and its inputs, method, outputs, and limitations recorded.
- **Reviewed:** A reviewer examined the work; identify the reviewer and scope.
- **Verified:** A specific claim or artifact was checked against specified evidence.
- **Validated:** Defined criteria were met through an appropriate validation process.
- **Implemented:** A change was actually made.
- **Authorized:** A competent authority explicitly approved the relevant action.

A tabletop scenario, narrative walkthrough, or description is not an executed simulation. Research does not itself authorize implementation.

For each formal pass record date, scope, questions, methods, sources, source versions, assumptions, findings, contradictions, limitations, corrections, open questions, and next steps. Preserve source URLs and access dates. Use version control for corrections rather than silently replacing earlier conclusions.

## 12. Current programme limitations

The 1789–1797 and 1797–1825 working papers consolidate exploratory conversation research and linked sources. They are not exhaustive historical inventories or complete primary-source audits. Some findings remain hypotheses or require corroboration. No independent peer review, validated simulation, or external reproduction has been completed.

**Core method rule:** Reconstruct the decision environment before judging the decision; include the people who experienced its consequences; and keep conclusions open to correction.
