# Working Paper: 1797–1825 — Revolution, Expansion, War, and Constitutional Conflict

**Programme:** GC-R02 — Historical Decision-Making and Human Agency  
**Period:** 1797–1825  
**Presidential administrations:** John Adams, Thomas Jefferson, James Madison, James Monroe  
**Status:** Preliminary working paper; evidence gathering and corroboration continue  
**Document date:** 2026-11-02

## 1. Scope and global context

This paper examines U.S. presidential decisions within the wider world of the French Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars, the Haitian Revolution, independence movements in Spanish America, imperial competition, and major social and economic change.

Relevant global developments include:

- The French Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars (1792–1815).
- Haitian independence in 1804 following the Haitian Revolution.
- The White Lotus Rebellion in Qing China (1796–1804).
- The Fourth Anglo-Mysore War (1798–1799) and East India Company expansion in South Asia.
- British abolition of its international slave trade in 1807, without abolition of slavery throughout the British Empire at that time.
- Spanish American independence movements, especially after the Napoleonic invasion of Spain in 1808.
- The independence of Brazil in 1822 and Portugal's recognition in 1825.

These events must be divided into (a) developments demonstrably connected to U.S. decisions, (b) global contextual developments, and (c) comparative cases. Do not infer a causal connection merely because events overlapped in time.

## 2. John Adams (1797–1801)

### Quasi-War with France

French seizures of American merchant vessels, deteriorating diplomatic relations, and the XYZ Affair generated pressure for confrontation. Adams supported military preparation and permitted naval hostilities but continued diplomatic efforts rather than seeking a formal declaration of war.

The Convention of 1800 (Treaty of Mortefontaine) was concluded on September 30, 1800. Its ratification process extended into 1801. It ended the formal state of conflict and addressed the prior treaties between the countries.

Research determinants:
- Attacks on American commerce and maritime rights.
- Treaty obligations to France.
- Domestic pressure from Federalists and public opinion.
- Limited American military capacity.
- Economic dependence on international trade.
- Adams's decision to continue diplomacy despite pressure for escalation.

Sources: [Convention of 1800 (Yale Avalon)](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/19th_century/fr1800.asp); [U.S. State Department, historical overview of the Quasi-War](https://history.state.gov/).

### Alien and Sedition Acts (1798)

The four laws were enacted by a Federalist-controlled Congress and signed by Adams. They increased naturalization residency requirements, expanded presidential authority over certain noncitizens, addressed alien enemies during war or threatened invasion, and criminalized specified forms of criticism of the federal government.

The acts must be distinguished individually: their provisions and duration differed. The Sedition Act expired in 1801; the Alien Enemies Act remained part of federal law. The acts were used against Democratic-Republican newspaper editors.

Research determinants:
- Anticipated war with France.
- Federalist concerns about foreign influence.
- Partisan conflict.
- Beliefs that criticism could undermine national security.
- Executive and legislative choices about the limits of dissent.

Source: [Alien and Sedition Acts (National Archives)](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/alien-and-sedition-acts).

### Haiti and U.S. policy

The Adams administration developed a working relationship with Toussaint Louverture's forces during the Haitian Revolution. Jefferson's administration subsequently adopted a more restrictive posture toward Haiti.

The full extent, legal basis, implementation, and political determinants of this change require additional documentation. Distinguish trade policy, military assistance, diplomatic recognition, and formal treaty relations. The United States did not formally recognize Haiti until 1862.

Sources for further research: [Haitian Revolution, Duke Haiti Lab](https://sites.duke.edu/haitilab/english/haitian-revolution/); [Haitian Declaration of Independence, 1804 translation (Duke)](https://today.duke.edu/showcase/haitideclaration/declarationstext.html); [Haitian Declaration of Independence (Encyclopedia Virginia)](https://encyclopediavirginia.org/primary-documents/the-haitian-declaration-of-independence-january-1-1804/).

## 3. Thomas Jefferson (1801–1809)

### Louisiana Purchase (1803)

The treaty with France was signed April 30, 1803. The United States agreed to pay $15 million and acquired a vast territory west of the Mississippi River.

Jefferson generally favored strict constitutional interpretation and questioned whether the Constitution explicitly authorized the federal government to acquire territory. His correspondence shows concern about the constitutional basis and the need for additional information about the territory.

The treaty was negotiated between the United States and France. It did not itself establish that Indigenous nations living in the territory had consented to transfer their sovereignty or lands.

Research determinants:
- American access to New Orleans and the Mississippi.
- French and Napoleonic strategic calculations.
- Risk of renewed war with Britain.
- Jefferson's constitutional concerns.
- Incomplete information about the acquired territory.
- The interests and sovereignty of Indigenous nations.

Sources: [Louisiana Purchase Treaty (National Archives)](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/louisiana-purchase-treaty); [Jefferson to John Breckinridge, August 12, 1803 (Founders Online copy)](https://teachingamericanhistory.org/library/document/letter-to-john-breckinridge/); [Jefferson's communications on Louisiana, National Archives DocsTeach](https://docsteach.org/document/consent-louisiana-purchase/).

### Barbary conflict

Jefferson faced disputes with Tripoli concerning American shipping, tribute, and maritime protection. The United States used naval force during the conflict. A Treaty of Peace and Amity was signed at Tripoli on June 4, 1805; the Senate advised and consented to ratification in 1806.

Research must distinguish diplomatic negotiations, congressional authorizations, naval operations, ransom, and the political structures of North African states. Do not use a single label such as piracy to erase the diplomatic context or the practices of other maritime powers.

Source: [Treaty of Peace and Amity with Tripoli, 1805 (Yale Avalon)](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/19th_century/bar1805t.asp).

### Embargo Act (1807)

Jefferson signed an embargo prohibiting American vessels from departing for foreign ports. The measure attempted to use economic pressure to protect neutral commerce and avoid war with Britain and France. It caused substantial disruption to American commercial activity and required significant enforcement.

Research determinants:
- British and French interference with neutral trade.
- Fear of military escalation.
- Belief in the leverage of American commerce.
- Underestimation of domestic economic effects.
- Enforcement and smuggling incentives.

Source: [Embargo Act of December 22, 1807 (GovInfo, Statutes at Large)](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/STATUTE-2/STATUTE-2-Pg451-3).

### International slave trade

Jefferson signed the legislation prohibiting the importation of enslaved people effective January 1, 1808. This prohibited the international importation under U.S. law; it did not abolish domestic slavery or the interstate slave trade, and illegal trafficking continued.

Further research must distinguish the law's text, enforcement, evasion, and lived consequences.

## 4. James Madison (1809–1817)

### War of 1812

Madison's June 1, 1812 message to Congress identified maritime impressment, blockades, interference with neutral commerce, and British involvement in frontier conflict as grievances. Congress declared war on June 18, 1812.

The British Orders in Council were revoked in June 1812, but news of the repeal did not reach the United States before Congress voted. The timing and communication delays must be incorporated into any assessment of the decision.

Research determinants:
- Maritime rights and impressment.
- British and French policies during the Napoleonic Wars.
- Congressional divisions.
- War preparedness and military capacity.
- Frontier conflict and Indigenous sovereignty.
- Political pressure and diplomatic timing.

Sources: [Madison's June 1, 1812 war message (Miller Center)](https://millercenter.org/the-presidency/presidential-speeches/june-1-1812-special-message-congress-foreign-policy-crisis-war); [Madison's message (American Presidency Project)](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/special-message-887).

### Tecumseh and Indigenous confederacy

Tecumseh and Tenskwatawa helped organize Indigenous resistance to territorial dispossession. Tecumseh sought collective political action and challenged the legitimacy of individual land cessions affecting multiple nations.

His negotiations with William Henry Harrison, the attack on Prophetstown in 1811, the War of 1812, and Tecumseh's death in 1813 should be studied through Indigenous and American sources.

Avoid treating Indigenous communities as a single unified actor. Examine their internal political differences, alliances, and distinct objectives.

### Creek War and Treaty of Fort Jackson (1814)

The Creek War involved Red Stick resistance and divisions among Creek communities. U.S. forces also received assistance from Cherokee and Choctaw allies. The Treaty of Fort Jackson imposed extensive territorial cessions following the defeat of the Red Stick forces.

The cessions affected Creek communities beyond those directly involved in fighting. This case requires examination of military coercion, treaty authority, the positions of allied communities, and long-term territorial consequences.

### African Americans and the war

Free African Americans served in military and maritime roles. Enslaved people also escaped to British forces and sought freedom through wartime opportunities. These choices must be treated as acts of agency under coercive conditions, not merely as incidental consequences of military operations.

## 5. James Monroe (1817–1825)

### First Seminole War (1817–1818)

General Andrew Jackson entered Spanish Florida during the First Seminole War and seized Spanish positions. British subjects Alexander Arbuthnot and Robert Ambrister were tried by military proceedings and executed.

Monroe's administration and Secretary of State John Quincy Adams defended aspects of Jackson's conduct. The precise scope of Jackson's instructions, his independent discretion, the legality of the executions, the Spanish response, and the cabinet's internal deliberations remain central research questions.

The Adams-Onís Treaty was signed February 22, 1819, and ratifications were exchanged in 1821. Spain ceded East and West Florida to the United States, while the treaty settled other territorial claims and boundaries.

Sources: [Adams-Onís Treaty, 1819 (Yale Avalon)](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/19th_century/sp1819.asp); [Treaty of 1819, text and ratification details](https://ylsavalonprod.azurewebsites.net/19th_century/sp1819.asp).

### Monroe Doctrine (1823)

Monroe's December 2, 1823 annual message warned European powers against further colonization and intervention in the Western Hemisphere. John Quincy Adams influenced the policy and resisted a proposed joint declaration with Britain.

Research must examine the doctrine's stated principles, contemporary geopolitical conditions, American capacity to enforce it, and its implications for newly independent Latin American nations.

Source: [Monroe Doctrine, December 2, 1823 (National Archives)](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/monroe-doctrine).

### Missouri Compromise (1820)

The legislation admitted Missouri as a slave state and Maine as a free state and prohibited slavery in the remainder of the Louisiana Territory north of latitude 36°30′, subject to its terms.

The compromise preserved a political balance while leaving slavery intact and extending the geographic reach of the conflict over slavery. The experiences of enslaved people and the political interests of free Black communities must remain part of the analysis.

Source: [Missouri Compromise, 1820 (National Archives)](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/missouri-compromise).

## 6. Cross-period research questions

These cases raise several questions that should guide further investigation:

1. When does national security become a pretext for suppressing lawful criticism?
2. How should decision-makers respond when economic policy imposes disproportionate costs on less powerful communities?
3. What makes a treaty legitimate when parties have unequal military and bargaining power?
4. When does a military operation exceed the authority granted to its commander?
5. How should governments respond when their stated principles conflict with their material interests?
6. How should a system represent people who are affected by decisions but excluded from the decision-making process?
7. How can collective progress preserve the autonomy and rights of individuals?

## 7. Research status

This paper is a source-linked synthesis and a guide to the next documentary work. It does not claim that each case has received a full archival audit. The source register should be expanded with original correspondence, congressional journals, treaty records, court proceedings, and specialist scholarship.

No historical simulation has been executed. No independent review or validation has been completed.

**Core conclusion:** Historical decisions must be understood through their context, the information available, the people who influenced them, the alternatives considered, and the experiences of all those affected.
